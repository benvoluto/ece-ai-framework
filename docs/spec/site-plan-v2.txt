WATCH"Back office is safe" is the one shortcut this
framework must not take. In early childhood the
back office is where the child data lives — subsidy
billing contains family income, immigration-adjacent
documentation, and custody information; grant
narratives routinely contain child vignettes. Tier 1
has to be defined by the absence of child and family
data, not by the word "administrative."
05Principles
Seven, named as one word plus a claim in the
imperative so they survive being quoted in a board
meeting. Two of these exist in no other AI
framework anywhere, and those two are why this
site is not a link to the K-12 guidance.
RELATIONSHIP Use AI to return adult
attention to children.
Development from birth to five is
built through serve-and-return
interaction with a consistent,
attuned adult. Anything that

occupies the adult's attention or
substitutes for the interaction hits
the mechanism of development
directly. This inverts the usual
efficiency argument into a
pedagogical one: the strongest
case for AI on paperwork is that
paperwork is time stolen from
children. Zero to Three's
appropriate-use recommendation
for educators is exactly this —
administrative tasks and planning,
supporting adults' time rather than
replacing human caregivers.
DEVELOPMENT Judge every tool against what
a child this age actually needs.
No other AI framework has a
developmental-appropriateness
test, because every one of them
was written for K-12 or for generic
nonprofits. RAND found the
dominant hesitation among pre-K
teachers is developmental
appropriateness and screen time
— not privacy. Meeting the field
where its actual concern lives is
both honest and strategically
smart. Drafting note: the AAP
replaced its prescriptive time limits
with the "5 C's" framework in
January 2026 — do not restate the
2016 hour limits, which most ECE
materials still cite.

STANDING Decide for children who
cannot decide for themselves.
A four-year-old cannot notice an AI
system, object to it, recognize it as
non-human, or report harm from it,
and leaves no record of dissent.
This is qualitatively different from
K-12, where student voice is at
least available in principle.
Parental consent is the only
consent available and it is
structurally compromised: signed
at enrollment as a condition of
care, often by families with no
alternative provider, sometimes
under subsidy conditions,
frequently in a second language.
The National Center for Youth
Law's digital legacy framing
belongs here — data created
about a three-year-old in 2026
may follow them for sixty years.
CUSTODY Know where a child's record
goes, and be able to get it back.
Head Start's 45 CFR 1303.24
already requires disclosure logs.
§1303.23(e) gives parents an
unqualified right to review any
written agreement the program
has with a third party — the text is
not limited to agreements naming
their child, which makes it the
strongest existing hook for a
parent asking to see an AI vendor

parent asking to see an AI vendor
contract. IDEA gives parents a
right to have records destroyed
once no longer needed — a right
FERPA alone does not confer, and
one an AI vendor that has ingested
IFSP text into a retained
embedding store cannot honor.
And the amended COPPA Rule
requires separate verifiable
parental consent for disclosing
children's personal information to a
third party for AI development,
which the FTC has said is not
"integral" to a service and so
cannot ride on general consent.
Note the gap: the Rule is quieter
about an operator training its own
models on data it already holds.
That is a contract term to write, not
a law to cite.
CONSEQUENCE No AI output decides
anything about a child.
This generalizes rules Head Start
programs already live under.
§1302.33 prohibits using
screening or assessment to
exclude children from enrollment,
or to rank, compare, or evaluate
individual children for rewards or
sanctions. §1302.17 prohibits
expulsion for behavior.
Generalized: no AI output may be
the basis for excluding,
disenrolling, suspending, or

disenrolling, suspending, or
expelling a child, or for a special
education referral, without an
independent human determination
on the underlying evidence.
Anchoring the guardrail in existing

LANGUAGE Serve the third of children who
are learning two languages.
There are 7 .1 million dual language
learners ages 0–5 — a third of all
young children, and more than
20% of the young-child population
in over half of all states. They are
not a special case; they are the
population. Every AI failure mode
compounds for them: speech
recognition performs worse on
accented and child speech,
screening instruments require
cultural and linguistic adaptation,
translated consent forms are
where informed consent quietly
fails. §1302.102(c)(2) already
requires DLL-disaggregated
analysis, so the framework can
require DLL-disaggregated
performance evidence from
vendors as an extension of an
existing duty rather than a new
ask.

CURRENCY Say when this was written
and what has changed since.
The field's flagship technology
position statement is fourteen
years old and predates the
smartphone-saturated classroom.
Meanwhile Colorado's AI Act was
delayed, blocked in federal court,
and replaced by a different statute
inside eighteen months. Every
artifact carries a date, a version,
and a changelog entry. Guidance
that cannot say when it was last
reviewed should not be trusted,
and saying so in your own
principles is what earns the right to
say it about the 2012 statement.
CUT CANDIDATEIf seven feels long, Currency is the
one to demote — it governs the framework rather
than the use of AI, and could live as a masthead
commitment instead. Six is probably the sweet spot.
06Where AI earns its place
A site that is only a list of prohibitions will be read
once and never returned to. Most of what AI can
do in early childhood is unglamorous, low-risk, and
worth real money to a business running on 1–3%
margins — and every hour it returns is an hour an

adult spends with children.
This is the Relationship principle made concrete,
and it is also the page that makes the bright lines
credible. A framework that only says no reads as
hostile to a director who is drowning; a framework
that says here is where this genuinely helps, and
here is the short list where it doesn't reads as
useful. Each entry below carries its risk tier and its
guardrail, because the point is not that these uses
are unregulated — it is that they are worth doing
carefully.

medication log, an expired CPR certification, and
playground surfacing depth. The corrective action plan
is due in ten business days. The director has done this
before. The fixing was the easy part; writing it up took
most of a weekend.
RETURNS The remedies still take what they take.
The document describing them drops
from a weekend to an evening. Across a
year — licensing renewal, two
monitoring visits, a policy manual refresh
— this is the difference between a
director who does paperwork on
Sundays and one who doesn't.
GUARDRAIL The findings and the remedies are hers;
only the drafting is the tool's. Corrective
action plans often reference specific

incidents, so use an approved tool with
an agreement on file and strip child

RETURNS This is not efficiency; it is revenue. On
margins this thin, recovering a few
hundred dollars a month of correctly

GUARDRAIL The most sensitive data in the building —

Finding the money you were always eligible for
TIER 1
A family child care provider has been in business eleven
years. In that time she has learned about exactly two
funding sources: CACFP, because a peer mentioned it,
and a state stabilization grant, because it arrived in the
mail. She has never heard of the facility improvement

fund her county runs, and she has never completed an

RETURNS A profile-matched list of what she is
actually eligible for — QRIS quality
incentives, CACFP, county and First 5

ten-minute conversation instead of a
blank page. The application she would
never have started gets submitted.
GUARDRAIL Verify every opportunity, amount, and
deadline against the source before
applying — a hallucinated deadline costs
a provider real money. And the moment a
narrative describes an individual child,
the work moves to Tier 2.
The empty slotTIER 2
A center runs at 87% enrollment. Two infant spots have
been open five weeks. Nine families sit on the waitlist,
but nobody has called them, because the person who
would make the calls is covering ratio in the toddler
room at three o'clock.
RETURNS Unfilled slots are the largest single
revenue leak in the sector, and how fast
an inquiry gets answered largely
determines whether it becomes a tour.
An assistant that responds within
minutes, works the waitlist, and books
tours converts a structural leak into filled

razor-thin margin.

THE RULE THAT KEEPS THIS PAGE HONEST
Every entry on the live page ends with what
the program actually did with the time —
hired a floater, gave a teacher a paid
planning hour, opened an infant slot,
stopped working Sundays. Without that, this
is an efficiency brochure, and the field has
plenty of those. With it, it is the Relationship

principle with receipts. Collect those
outcomes from real pilots rather than
borrowing vendor case studies, name no
products, and carry the tier and the
guardrail on every single one.
07Bright lines
A short prohibited-use list is usable; a five-by-five
impact-likelihood grid is not. Each line pairs a rule
with the legal or evidentiary basis that makes it
defensible, because a director will have to justify it
to an owner or a board.
Emotion, affect, or engagement recognition on
children.
EU AI Act Art. 5(1)(f) prohibits emotion inference in
education institutions outright — and it reaches placing
on the market and putting into service, not just end use,
so it binds vendors too. The mechanism is documented:
Halberstadt et al. (2020) found prospective teachers
1.36 times more likely to show racialized anger bias
toward Black children's faces. Human raters carry that
bias, training labels carry it, models learn it. There is no
U.S. analogue to the EU prohibition — which is precisely
why the field should write one.
AI as the basis for expelling, suspending,
disenrolling, or denying enrollment to a child.
Already law for Head Start under §1302.17 and

10The multi-site track

The design constraint that governs the rest of this
site — no lawyer, no IT department, twenty
minutes — is right for the modal provider and
wrong for a fourteen-site organization with an HR
director and outside counsel. Larger operators are
not a smaller problem. They are a di!erent one,
and the field has no guidance for them at all.
A large center, a chain, or a Head Start grantee
with delegate agencies has something the single
site does not: capacity to actually govern this.
What it does not have is anyone telling it how. Left
alone, these organizations fail in two opposite
directions, and both are worse than they look.
Failure mode one: the reflexive no
Risk-averse counsel or a cautious board issues a
blanket prohibition. It feels like the safe choice
and it is documented as a policy, so everyone
moves on.
What actually happens is that staff use AI anyway
— on personal phones, in free consumer accounts,
with child names in the prompt, outside any
register, any agreement, and any disclosure log.
The organization now has the same exposure it
had before, minus the ability to see it, plus a
written policy proving it knew better. A blanket
ban is not a safety posture. It is an unmonitored
one. This should be said plainly on the page,
because the people who need to hear it are exactly
the ones who believe they have solved the

problem.
The corollary matters too: a program that forbids
AI outright is choosing to leave the Section 06
savings on the table — the billing recovery, the
translated closure notices, the grant it never
applied for — while its staff take the risks anyway.
Failure mode two: the unmanaged yes
Scale cuts the other way just as easily. One site
pilots a camera vendor. Another turns on an
observation app's AI feature because it was on by
default. Procurement buys an enterprise chatbot
for the central office. Nobody has an inventory, no
two sites are operating to the same standard, and
governance debt compounds faster than at a
single site because there are more people able to
say yes.
What the multi-site track adds
Nine things, on top of everything in Section 09.
Each exists because it does not make sense below
a certain size.
1. A governance charter with real delegated
authority. Site directors approve Tier 1 without
asking. A named role approves Tier 2. A small
group approves Tier 3 in writing, with the
crosswalk in front of them. Without delegation
everything queues behind one person, and staff
route around the queue — which is how the
unmanaged yes starts.

2. One tool register, many sites. The single-site
register does not scale. One source of truth,
per-site adoption status, and a standing rule
that no site adopts a Tier 2 or Tier 3 tool that is
not on it.
3. Contract riders — because you actually have
leverage. This is the biggest asymmetry in the
sector and the most valuable thing large
operators can do. A single center can only ask
the ten questions. A fourteen-site organization,
or a grantee with delegates, can require terms:
no training on our data including internally, no
biometric processing without our written
instruction, deletion that reaches derived
artifacts, subprocessor notice, DLL-
disaggregated validation evidence on request,
and a notification commitment with a clock on
it. Publish model rider language. If the field's
larger buyers converge on the same terms,
vendor defaults shift for everyone — including
for the family child care provider with no
leverage at all. Frame it that way: this is a public
good only the big operators can supply.
4. A pilot protocol with a stop condition. Large
organizations should pilot, not roll out. One site,
a named owner, a fixed window, success criteria
declared in advance, staff and family notice,
and a real condition under which it ends. Most
enterprise AI pilots have no stop condition,
which is precisely why they never stop.
5. Multi-state compliance mapping. A chain
operating in Illinois, California, and New York

faces BIPA, ELPIPA, and Ed Law 2-d
simultaneously. Running different practices per
state is possible and almost always more
expensive than it looks; for most operators the
cheapest defensible path is a single standard
set at the strictest state's level. The crosswalk's
state layer matters more to this reader than to
any other.
6. Shadow-AI discovery, without punishing
anyone. Before writing policy, find out what is
already in use — a two-week amnesty window
where anyone can add a tool to the register
with no consequence. Skip this and the policy
governs a fiction.
7. Board and owner reporting on a cadence. A
standing quarterly item: tools in use by tier,
incidents, what changed, what is pending
approval. For Head Start this maps onto
existing Policy Council and governing body
reporting rather than adding a new obligation.
8. Role-differentiated training. A teacher, a family
services worker, and a billing clerk face
completely different risks from the same tool.
Large organizations can afford to train them
differently; small ones cannot. This is one of the
few places where scale is a genuine advantage
rather than a complication.
9. A named escalation path. Who does a teacher
call at four in the afternoon when a tool did
something strange? At a single site it is the
director. At fourteen sites it has to be a named
role, or it defaults to nobody.

HEAD START GRANTEES WITH DELEGATE AGENCIES

12Vendors
Write the questions as questions, not as criteria. A
single-site center has essentially no leverage with
a major vendor. What it can do is ask, record the
answer with a date, and choose between vendors
— and a field where five thousand directors ask

the same ten questions creates leverage no
individual director has.
1. Is any information we enter, or that your
product collects about children, used to train
or improve your models — or anyone else's? If
yes, under what consent?
2. Does your product compute face geometry,
voiceprints, or any other biometric identifier
from children? Including for photo tagging,
check-in, or search.
3. Does your product infer emotion, affect,
engagement, or behavioral risk about a child?
Ask directly; it is often buried under "insights."
4. If a family asks us to delete their child's
records, what exactly gets deleted — and does
that include anything derived from those
records? IDEA gives parents a destruction right
that most model architectures cannot honor.
5. Who are your subprocessors, and where is
children's data stored and processed?
6. Will you sign an agreement naming us as the
data controller, with your role limited to
processing on our instructions?
7. What is your written retention policy for
children's personal information, and where is it
published? Now a COPPA requirement.
8. For anything producing a judgment about a
child: what was it validated on, and was
performance analyzed separately for dual
language learners and children with

disabilities?
9. What happens to our data if we leave, and can
we export it in a usable format?
10. Who do we call when it produces something
wrong about a child, and what is your
notification commitment if something goes
wrong on your end?
The disclosure card, and eventually the register
A standardized one-page vendor disclosure
answering those ten questions in a fixed format,
published by the vendor, plus a public register of
tools that have filed one. The model exists in
healthcare and nowhere else; ECE directors
currently evaluate vendors with nothing.
This is achievable at small scale — twenty vendors
covers most of the market — and it would
immediately become the site's most-linked asset.
It also creates the only real incentive structure
available: filing is voluntary, but a director
comparing two products will notice which one
didn't.

16Decisions for you
Q1Who convenes?
LIIF has the ECE portfolio and provider relationships
but is a CDFI, not a standards body — a strength for
delivery and a question mark for neutrality. Is LIIF the
convener, a founding member, or the delivery
partner?
Q2Do vendors sit on the body?
It determines whether the disclosure card is viable
and whether the framework reaches providers

through the products they already use. It also
determines the first line of every critique. There is no
neutral answer, only a disclosed one.
Q3Bright lines as prohibitions, or as "requires
board-level approval"?
Prohibitions are usable and quotable. Some partners
will want softer language, especially on cameras,
where programs have liability reasons for wanting
them. My read: hold the prohibitions and lose the
partners who need them softened — a framework
that permits everything with paperwork is what the
field already has.
Q4Does the site take a position on specific
products?
The register implies it eventually will, at least by
omission. That is a meaningfully different institution
— and a different legal posture — than one that
publishes questions and lets directors ask them.
Q5What is the Spanish-language commitment,
exactly?
Simultaneous release, or translated later? Given the
Language principle, "later" undercuts the whole
thing. A resourcing question disguised as a
production question.
Q6Who owns legal review, and can it be pro bono?
The crosswalk is the site's crown jewel and cannot
ship on inference. A law school clinic or a firm's pro
bono practice is the natural fit, and having one

named on the page is itself a legitimacy asset.
17Sources & verification
Everything asserted here traces to a source. Two
categories matter differently: the durable evidence
base, and the items with a short shelf life that
must be re-checked immediately before anything
goes public.
Verify before publishing
The COPPA AI-consent boundary. The
separate-consent requirement carries an
express carve-out for disclosures "integral to
the nature of the service," and the FTC's AI
statement addresses disclosures to third
parties. Do not write that AI training "always"
requires separate consent — a vendor's
counsel will catch it, and the internal-training
gap is real.
Colorado. SB 24-205 was delayed to June
2026, then enforcement was blocked in federal
court in April 2026, then replaced by SB 26-189
in May 2026 with a notice-based regime
effective January 2027 . Source is a single law-
firm alert. Check the enrolled bill and the
docket.
AAP screen-time guidance. The 2026 "5 C's"
framework replaced the prescriptive hour

limits, but this rests on secondary coverage.
Confirm specifically what replaced the under-
24-month recommendation before writing
anything about infants.
Head Start rulemaking. An NPRM published
May 2026 proposes removing wage-
comparability and benefits requirements;
records and confidentiality provisions were not
proposed for change. Whether a final rule has
issued is unconfirmed.
Expulsion data vintage. The multipliers rest on
Gilliam's 2005 work and the suspension
disparity on the 2013–14 CRDC. Pull the newest
CRDC cycle.
Product claims. Several AI feature descriptions
come from vendor marketing. Notably,
brightwheel's public learning page described
no AI features as of this research. Confirm
directly with any vendor before naming them on
a public page.
The 2016 Yale eye-tracking study is routinely
mischaracterized. It found teachers gazed
more at Black children, especially boys — but
on the vignette task there was no racial
disparity in suspension or expulsion
recommendations. Cite CRDC and Gilliam's
2005 and 2014 work for disparities; cite the
2016 study only for the attention finding.
Overclaiming here is the easiest way to be
discredited in front of an audience that knows
this literature.

Evidence gaps to state rather than fill
No national data on broadband, devices, or IT
support among ECE providers, especially home-
based. No current verified national turnover figure.
No meta-analysis quantifying differential
screening-instrument accuracy by race or
language. No verified U.S. emotion-recognition
product in an ECE setting — the category is
arriving through general school-security AI. No
survey of AI use among Head Start grantees
outside districts, private centers, or family child
care homes; the RAND figures cover public-school
pre-K only.
