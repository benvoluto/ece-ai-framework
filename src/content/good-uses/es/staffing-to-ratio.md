The schedule is a constraint problem that changes every week. Ratios are legally
binding and they move — by age group, by the hour, by which children are
actually in the building at two o'clock on a Wednesday, by who called in sick at
six in the morning. The children do not arrive evenly. The infant room is at
ratio all day; the preschool room is at half its census before nine and after
four.

So the director does what anyone does with a problem too fiddly to solve by
hand: she solves it on the safe side. She puts an extra person on the floor from
seven to six, and absorbs the cost, because the alternative — being one adult
short when a licensor walks in — is a citation. On a one to three percent
margin, a couple of unnecessary staffed hours a day is the whole margin.

Building the schedule against the real arrival and departure curve is arithmetic
a tool is good at and Sunday night is not. Understaffing is a citation;
overstaffing is a margin killer; the gap between them is where both the money
and the Sunday nights are.

Two limits matter. Staff schedules are employment data about people, so the
tool proposes and the director decides. And this must not drift. The same data
that optimises a schedule can be turned into a productivity score on a workforce
earning close to minimum wage that never agreed to be measured that way. Keep it
a scheduling tool.
