The licensor leaves at four. By four-fifteen the director already knows what she
is going to do about all three findings: reprint the medication log with a
signature column, get two staff recertified in cardiopulmonary resuscitation
(CPR) on Saturday, and call the surfacing contractor who did the toddler yard
last spring.

None of that is the hard part. The hard part is the document — ten business days
to produce a corrective action plan that states each finding, the regulation it
cites, the specific remedy, the responsible person, the completion date and the
step that keeps it from recurring, in the register a licensing office expects.
She has written this document before. It takes a weekend, and the weekend comes
out of the only two days she is not in a classroom.

This is the narrowest possible use of a tool: she supplies the findings and the
remedies in her own words, and the tool assembles them into the structure the
plan requires. She reads every line, changes what is wrong, and signs it. The
fixing was always hers. Only the typing moved.
