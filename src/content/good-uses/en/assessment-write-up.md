Half a day on site: the coach walks the building, reads the policies, goes
through the books with the owner, and scores a Business Administration Scale
assessment item by item. That half day is the part that requires ten years of
experience. It is also, in staff time, the smaller half of the job.

Then come two days at a keyboard turning scored items into a narrative report a
provider can act on. The judgments were all made on site. What remains is
assembly — restating each item, attaching the evidence, writing the
recommendation in the house format, and producing something presentable.

Cutting write-up time by roughly two-thirds does not make any single report
better. It changes how many providers a fixed number of coaches can reach in a
year, which is the binding constraint on nearly every intermediary in this
field.

What has to stay fixed is the direction of authorship. The coach's scores and
judgments are the input; the tool assembles the document around them. It never
generates a rating, never infers a score from surrounding text, and never
reaches the provider without a person having read it end to end. These reports
name a real business and quote real financials, so this is approved-tool,
agreement-on-file work.
