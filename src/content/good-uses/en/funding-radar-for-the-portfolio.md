The information asymmetry runs the right way and still does no good. A Community
Development Financial Institution (CDFI) with 300 providers in its portfolio
hears about a county facility fund, a state quality incentive round and a USDA
program months before any individual provider does. What it has never had is a
way to say which of the 300 each one is for.

So it sends a newsletter. Eleven opportunities, most of which apply to almost
nobody reading, arriving on the fifteenth of the month in an inbox that is
already a licensing renewal and four parent emails deep. Open rates are what
open rates are.

A matched alert is a different object: this provider, this opportunity, this
deadline, these three documents you already have on file. Application rates rise
when the ask is specific, because the provider no longer has to do the triage
the intermediary was better placed to do anyway.

Two limits. Match on characteristics the provider has actually shared — license
type, county, size, what they told you they want to do — and never on inferred
ones, which is how a matching system starts making quiet judgments about who is
worth telling. And verify every opportunity, amount and deadline against the
source before it goes out. A wrong deadline costs the provider real money and
costs the intermediary the trust that made the alert worth opening.
