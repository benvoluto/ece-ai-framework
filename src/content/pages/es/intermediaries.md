<h2 class="govuk-heading-m" id="why-a-separate-track">Why this is a separate track</h2>

Everything else on this site is written for a director or a family child care
provider deciding what to do about AI inside her own program. This page is
written for the organizations around her: a community development financial
institution (CDFI) that lends to child care businesses, a resource and referral
agency (R&R, or CCR&R), a training and technical assistance (T&TA) provider, a
coaching network, a shared services alliance.

They need their own track for two reasons, and the first one is the important
one.

An intermediary's AI use makes decisions **about providers**. A provider's AI
use makes decisions about children and families. Both matter, but they are
different kinds of risk with different remedies. When a center director gets a
tool wrong, the person exposed is a child in her care, and the protections are
the ones the rest of this site describes — consent, custody of data, a human
determination before anything consequential happens to a child. When an
intermediary gets a tool wrong, the person exposed is the provider herself, and
what is at stake is credit, coaching, a referral, a place in a portfolio. She
is rarely in the room when that decision is made, and she usually cannot see
the reasoning. That asymmetry is what this page exists to govern.

The second reason is practical. Intermediaries are the delivery channel for
everything else here. The R&R network already runs business and compliance
technical assistance and touches thousands of providers; it reaches directors
this site will never reach on its own. An organization that hands providers a
policy about careful AI adoption is going to be asked, sooner or later, what it
did when AI reached its own consequential decisions. Answering that question
well is a condition of being able to deliver the rest.

<h2 class="govuk-heading-m" id="where-ai-helps">Where AI helps in intermediary work</h2>

Four of the twelve constructive uses on this site are intermediary uses. They
are set out in full on
<a class="govuk-link" href="/good-uses#for-intermediaries">Where AI helps</a>, each with its risk tier, its
guardrail and the principle it answers to. In short:

- <a class="govuk-link" href="/good-uses#coaching-notes-at-scale">What the coaching notes have been saying all along</a>
  — Tier 2. Years of technical assistance notes read as a set for the first
  time: which topics recur, which cluster with later closure, what providers
  keep asking for that the organization does not offer. Qualitative coding at a
  scale that was never affordable.
- <a class="govuk-link" href="/good-uses#assessment-write-up">The assessment write-up</a> — Tier 2. The
  business coach's scores and judgments stay the coach's; the tool assembles
  the document around them, and write-up time falls by roughly two-thirds. The
  same staff reach more providers.
- <a class="govuk-link" href="/good-uses#funding-radar-for-the-portfolio">A funding radar for the whole portfolio</a>
  — Tier 1. Matched alerts instead of a monthly newsletter nobody opens: this
  provider, this opportunity, this deadline, these documents.
- <a class="govuk-link" href="/good-uses#where-to-send-the-coach">Where to send the coach next</a> — Tier 3.
  A ranked view of where technical assistance would do the most good, built
  from signals the organization already holds. This is the one that needs the
  strongest guardrail on the page, and the next section is mostly about it.

<h2 class="govuk-heading-m" id="the-standard">The standard for the intermediary's own AI</h2>

Three areas of intermediary work reach decisions about a named provider:
lending and underwriting, portfolio monitoring, and coaching or technical
assistance triage. Each gets a standard below. One requirement runs through all
three, so it is stated once here: **every provider can see what the system says
about her, and can contest it.** A score, a rank or a flag that a provider
cannot see is not a tool she can correct, and the organizations holding these
systems are the ones best placed to be wrong about her quietly.

<h3 class="govuk-heading-s" id="lending">Lending and underwriting</h3>

This is the sharpest case and it is worked through in full as
<a class="govuk-link" href="/cases#cdfi-underwriting-model">a case</a>. A CDFI is offered an AI underwriting
model to speed up child care facility lending. The appeal is genuine — the
deals are small, the underwriting is slow, and faster decisions mean more
providers financed.

What is at stake is that these are credit decisions about providers who are
disproportionately women of color running micro-businesses, made with data
that encodes the sector's existing inequities. Home-based providers, newer
providers and providers in child care deserts are exactly the thin-file
borrowers a model will treat worst — not because they are worse credits, but
because there is less recorded about them.

Four requirements, before a pilot rather than after it:

1. **Fair lending review before the pilot**, not as a compliance sign-off once
   the model is already scoring applications.
2. **Adverse-action explainability.** A provider who is denied gets a specific
   and accurate reason. "The model declined it" is not a reason.
3. **Disparate-impact testing on the actual portfolio** — this lender's own
   borrowers, not the vendor's benchmark population.
4. **A human decision on every denial.** The model may inform; a person decides
   and is accountable for the decision.

And one more that is about standing rather than about the model: **publish the
practice standard, so the providers being underwritten can see it.** A lending
standard that only the lender can read asks borrowers to trust a process they
are not allowed to inspect.

<h3 class="govuk-heading-s" id="portfolio-monitoring">Portfolio monitoring</h3>

Reading an archive of coaching notes as a set is a genuine research gain, and
it is also the point at which a portfolio-level finding can quietly become a
file on an individual. Two rules keep it honest.

Notes carry provider identities and sometimes family identities. De-identify
before analysis, or work in an approved environment with an agreement in place
— this is approved-tool, agreement-on-file work, not something to paste into a
consumer chatbot.

Findings describe patterns across a portfolio. They never quietly become a
score attached to an individual provider. If a portfolio analysis starts
producing per-provider risk ratings, it has changed into something else and
needs to be governed as the triage systems below are governed, with the
disclosure and contest rights that go with them.

The same discipline applies to matching work such as the funding radar. Match
on business characteristics the provider has actually shared, never on inferred
ones, and verify every opportunity before it goes out: a wrong deadline costs a
provider money and costs the intermediary the trust that made the service worth
having.

<h3 class="govuk-heading-s" id="triage">Coaching and technical assistance triage</h3>

A network with 400 providers and eight coaches has a real allocation problem,
and triage today is whoever called, whoever a coach happened to visit, and
whoever a funder asked about. A ranked view of where assistance would do the
most good is a defensible thing to want. It is also the closest thing on this
site to a bright line for an intermediary, and it needs the strongest guardrail
here.

- **It may route support. It may never route sanctions, funding denials or
  portfolio exits.** The moment a triage score is used to withdraw something
  from a provider rather than to offer something to her, it has become an
  adverse decision system and the lending requirements above apply to it.
- **Every provider can see what the system says about her and can contest it.**
  Disclosure is not optional here, and a contest has to reach a person with the
  authority to change the record.
- **Test the model for who it ranks as less worth helping.** Specifically:
  whether it systematically ranks home-based providers, newer providers, or
  providers in child care deserts lower. Test before deployment and again on
  live results.

The last one deserves the plainest possible statement, because it is the
failure that will actually happen. The thin-file problem is real here, and it
points the wrong way. The providers an organization holds the least data about
— the newest, the smallest, the ones in the places with the fewest services —
will look like the lowest-signal cases to a ranking model, and they are very
often the ones where a coach's time would matter most. A system that quietly
sends eight coaches toward the providers who are already best documented has
made the sector's inequities more efficient.

<h2 class="govuk-heading-m" id="co-branding">Co-branding: delivering the provider artifacts under your own name</h2>

The artifacts on this site are meant to be delivered by intermediaries, not
only downloaded by directors. A provider is far more likely to adopt a policy
her coach walked her through than one she found on a website, and the
organizations that already run business and compliance technical assistance are
the ones with the relationship to do it. Put your name on them.

What you may change:

- Your organization's name, logo and contact details, on any artifact.
- The cover note, the framing, and the order in which you introduce them.
- Your state's specifics — licensing terminology, subsidy program names, the
  state privacy law that applies to your providers.
- The format: what ships as a six-page policy for a center can be delivered as
  a one-page signed document for a family child care home. Length and reading
  level are meant to move with the provider's capacity.

What does not change when you co-brand:

- **The principles and the bright lines.** They are the same for a family child
  care home, a center and a multi-site operator, and they stay the same when an
  intermediary delivers them. Everything else about an artifact is adaptable;
  these are not. An artifact that softens a bright line is no longer this
  framework, and should not carry a joint name.
- **The provenance.** Every prohibition here carries the statute, regulation or
  evidence that makes it defensible, because a director will have to justify it
  to an owner or a board. Do not strip the authorities out to shorten the
  document.
- **The date and the version.** Each artifact carries its own last-updated
  stamp and version, and they travel with it into your branded copy. A provider
  needs to be able to tell which draft she signed, and you need to be able to
  tell her when it changes.
- **The working-draft status.** Everything here is an unreviewed working draft.
  Say so in your version too. Delivering it under your name does not make it
  reviewed, and a provider who later learns otherwise has learned it from the
  wrong direction.

One thing to do while you deliver: ask the programs you work with to send
back the policy they actually adopted. Nobody in the field knows what
providers' AI policies really say, and the organizations delivering the
artifacts are the only ones positioned to find out. Collect that with the
provider's permission and it becomes a revision input for the next version.

<h2 class="govuk-heading-m" id="the-artifact">The Intermediary AI Practice Standard</h2>

The standard described on this page ships as one of the sixteen artifacts: the
**Intermediary AI Practice Standard**, for CDFIs, R&Rs and T&TA providers. It
covers how an intermediary handles AI in its own lending, underwriting,
portfolio monitoring and coaching, plus the co-branding guidance for delivering
the provider artifacts under its own name. Like every artifact here it is
editable, carries a last-updated stamp and has a Spanish version.

It is written to be published rather than filed. That is the point of the last
requirement in the lending section: the providers an intermediary underwrites,
monitors and coaches should be able to read the standard that governs those
decisions.
