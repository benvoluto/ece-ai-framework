Those two questions are kept apart on purpose. A page that mixes "here is what your program should do" with "here is what the legislature should pass" reads as advocacy to the provider and as vague to the legislator, and ends up useless to both. So nothing on this page is guidance for a provider, and nothing in the provider guidance depends on any of it being adopted.

Read this section as a set of drafted asks in an unreviewed working draft. No legislature, agency, licensing body or quality rating system has adopted any of it, none of it has been reviewed by counsel, and none of it has been endorsed by or is attributed to any organization. It is written down so that it can be argued with.

The asks were not drafted from a preference about technology. Each one closes a gap that is documented elsewhere on this site, in the regulatory crosswalk or the bright lines, and each is stated in the form the body it is addressed to actually works in. Where the underlying source has a short shelf life or an unresolved reading, this page says so rather than asserting the claim.

Start from the state of play, because it is more unusual than it sounds. No state has legislated on artificial intelligence in early childhood. FutureEd tracks 77 AI-in-education bills across 27 states this session; MultiState counts 134 across 31. Neither tracker contains a single pre-K, preschool, child care or Head Start provision. State education-department AI guidance is now widespread and it is entirely K-12-framed. What is moving fast is state law on chatbots, biometrics and minors' data — 14 chatbot laws enacted in the 2026 term alone — and all of it is written for adolescents. Child safety is also the one lane where state AI law is explicitly protected from federal preemption efforts, which makes obligations built on child safety and child privacy the most durable ground available to a legislator working here.

The second thing to hold on to is that legislation is not the main road. The path from a framework to a requirement in this sector runs through Child Care and Development Fund (CCDF) plans, provider agreements, licensing and quality rating and improvement systems (QRIS) — not through statute. That is why the asks below are sorted by the body that would carry them rather than by topic. Three of the four groups need no new statutory authority at all, and the two that reach every provider in a state, including family child care homes, are the two that a legislature never has to vote on.
