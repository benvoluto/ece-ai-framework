Development from birth to five is built through serve-and-return interaction
with a consistent, attuned adult. A child reaches, babbles or points; an adult
notices and answers; the child goes further. That exchange is not the setting in
which development happens. It is the mechanism by which it happens. So anything
that occupies the adult's attention, or that substitutes for the interaction
itself, is not a neutral addition to the room. It acts directly on the thing
that makes a young child develop.

That is why this framework does not treat efficiency as the argument for AI in
early childhood. It inverts the usual efficiency case into a pedagogical one.
The strongest reason to let a tool draft the newsletter, reformat the schedule
or translate the routine notice is not that it saves money. It is that
paperwork is time stolen from children, and this is a way to give some of it
back.

Zero to Three's appropriate-use recommendation for educators says the same
thing: administrative tasks and planning, supporting adults' time rather than
replacing human caregivers. Read that as the test to apply to any tool put in
front of you. A tool that hands an adult back to a child is doing the work this
principle asks for. A tool that stands between them is not, however much time
it appears to save.
