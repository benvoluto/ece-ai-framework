A four-year-old cannot notice an AI system, object to it, recognize it as
non-human, or report harm from it. She leaves no record of dissent, so there is
nothing later to point back to and say that someone raised a concern at the
time. This is qualitatively different from K-12, where student voice is at
least available in principle — an older student can ask what the tool is, refuse
to use it, or tell a parent that something felt wrong. In a room of
three-year-olds, no such signal will ever arrive. Silence here is not consent
and it is not comfort. It is the absence of any channel at all.

Parental consent is the only consent available, and it is structurally
compromised. It is signed at enrollment as a condition of care, often by families
with no alternative provider within reach, sometimes under subsidy conditions
that make refusal costly, and frequently in a second language. A form signed in
those circumstances is a real legal document and a weak ethical one. Treat it as
the floor, not as the answer.

Which is why the decision falls to the adult who runs the program. The
National Center for Youth Law's digital legacy framing belongs here: data
created about a three-year-old in 2026 may follow that person for sixty years,
into contexts no one in the room can picture today. The child has standing in
that future and no voice in the present. Someone has to hold it for her, and on
the day a tool is adopted that someone is you.
