No other AI framework has a developmental-appropriateness test, because every
one of them was written for K-12 schooling or for generic nonprofits. A
question that is sensible about a fifteen-year-old — does this help her learn,
can she opt out, does she understand what it is — carries almost nothing over
to a two-year-old. This principle is one of the two that make this site
something other than a link to the K-12 guidance. The test it sets is simple to
state and hard to pass: judge the tool against what a child of this age
actually needs, not against what it does for an adult or an older student.

It is also where the field's real concern lives. RAND found that the dominant
hesitation among pre-K teachers is developmental appropriateness and screen
time — not privacy. Any framework that opens with data protection is answering
a question the workforce did not ask first. Meeting the field where its concern
actually sits is both the honest thing to do and the reason this guidance has a
chance of being used.

One drafting note, carried here rather than buried, because the guidance moved
recently. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) replaced its prescriptive
time limits with the "5 C's" framework in January 2026. Most early childhood
materials still cite the 2016 hour limits, and this framework does not restate
them. But our account of the 5 C's rests on secondary coverage rather than on
the AAP's own publication, and what replaced the recommendation for children
under twenty-four months has not been confirmed. Until it is, this framework
says nothing specific about infants and screens, and you should treat any
second-hand version of the new guidance — including this one — as needing a
check against the source.
