The pitch is about liability, and the liability worry is real. A camera that
shows what happened in a disputed thirty seconds has settled more than one
complaint in a program's favour. That is the security question, and it has a
clear answer: recorded video, retained briefly, reviewed by a person when there
is a reason to review it.

The analytics are a different purchase, sold in the same conversation. "Detects
policy violations" means a model is watching the room continuously and forming
judgments — about children who cannot consent to being watched at all, and
about a workforce that is 40–56% women of color earning very little. Those
judgments do not stay inside the software. They become licensing complaints,
they become write-ups and terminations, and in the worst case they become the
paper trail behind a child's removal from the program.

So the first move is to pull the two apart and price them separately. Ask what
the camera costs without the analytics. The answer tells you a great deal about
what is actually being sold.

Then, if you are still considering the analytics, this is Tier 3 due diligence —
all ten vendor questions, answers in writing, dated. Two of them carry most of
the weight here. Question 3 asks directly whether the product infers emotion,
affect, engagement or behavioral risk about a child; ask it plainly, because
this capability is usually described as "insights" rather than as emotion
recognition. Question 8 asks what any judgment-producing component was
validated on, and whether performance was analyzed separately for dual language
learners and children with disabilities.

If any component infers affect, emotion or engagement on children, that is
bright line 1 and the answer is no. Not a mitigation, not a setting you leave
off — no. Article 5(1)(f) of the EU AI Act prohibits emotion inference in
education institutions outright, and it reaches placing on the market and
putting into service, not only end use, so it binds vendors and not only
buyers. The mechanism is documented: Halberstadt and colleagues (2020) found
prospective teachers 1.36 times more likely to show racialised anger bias
toward Black children's faces. Human raters carry that bias, training labels
carry it, and models learn it. There is no U.S. equivalent of the EU
prohibition, which is exactly why the field should write one.

Get the prohibition into the contract in your own words — that no affect,
emotion or engagement inference runs on children, at any time, in any module,
including in features released later. A verbal assurance from a sales
representative is not a contract term, and the feature that triggers this line
is usually the one that ships next year.

**What we do not know.** We have not verified a U.S. emotion-recognition
product in use in an early childhood setting. The category is arriving through
general school-security AI rather than through early childhood products, which
is why this case is written about a pitch rather than about a deployment.
