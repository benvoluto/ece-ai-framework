A chain operating in Illinois, California and New York faces the Illinois
Biometric Information Privacy Act (BIPA), California's Early Learning Personal
Information Protection Act (ELPIPA) and New York Education Law 2-d
simultaneously. Not in sequence, not whichever applies to headquarters — all
three, at once, across the same tool and often the same contract.

Running different practices per state is possible. It is also almost always more
expensive than it looks, and the cost lands in places the spreadsheet does not
anticipate: a consent form that differs by state and therefore has to be
maintained, trained on and audited three ways; a vendor agreement that has to be
negotiated three ways; a staff member who transfers between sites and carries the
wrong habits with her; and an error rate that rises with every additional
variant.

**For most operators the cheapest defensible path is a single standard set at
the strictest state's level.** One consent instrument, one set of contract terms,
one training. You will occasionally be doing more than a given state requires.
That is the point: the overshoot is cheaper than the variance, and it is far
easier to defend.

The state layer of the regulatory crosswalk matters more to this reader than to
any other on the site. A single-site provider reads one column. A multi-state
operator reads the crosswalk to find the strictest requirement on each dimension
and then writes the organization's standard at that line — which means the
crosswalk is not reference material for her, it is the input to a decision.
