Large organizations should pilot, not roll out. The capacity to try something at
one site and learn from it before it reaches thirteen others is a genuine
advantage of scale, and most organizations waste it by treating the first site
as a launch rather than a test.

A pilot worth the name has six things fixed before it starts:

- **One site.** Not a representative sample, not three sites to be fair. One, so
  that stopping is cheap.
- **A named owner.** A person, not a department, who is answerable for what the
  pilot found.
- **A fixed window.** A start date and an end date, both written down.
- **Success criteria declared in advance.** What would have to be true at the end
  for this to be worth extending — stated before anyone has an investment in the
  answer.
- **Staff and family notice.** The people whose work and whose children are
  inside the pilot are told that it is a pilot, what the tool does, and what
  information it touches.
- **A real condition under which it ends.** Not "we will review it", which is not
  a condition. A named circumstance that, if it occurs, stops the pilot.

The last one is the one that gets dropped, and it is the one that matters. Most
enterprise AI pilots have no stop condition, which is precisely why they never
stop: with no criterion for failure, a pilot that has not visibly exploded looks
like a success, and it converts into a rollout by inertia rather than by
decision. Write the stop condition down, give it to the named owner, and make
ending the pilot an ordinary outcome rather than an admission.
